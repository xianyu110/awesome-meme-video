## What is the Tweenslop trend, and where did it start?

The word joins **tweening**, the animation shortcut where software fills in the frames between two key poses, and **slop**, internet slang for cheap, mass-made content. Animators first used it as a put-down for lazy motion: one flat picture that slides, stretches and wobbles instead of being drawn frame by frame. In 2026 it turned into a style people make on purpose, mostly in Alight Motion and CapCut.

The look most edits copy comes from the animator CheezeDibbles (@wombadal): on 11 August 2026 a cut-out edit of snack cans and a cartoon character bouncing to funk passed 20 million views. "CheezeDibbles style" remakes followed, like @brian.boy1's on 26 September (2.3 million views), and in late October the "my tweening style vs tweenslop" format took over: @yashi.dark's comparison has 4.6 million views. By 14 November 2026 the #tweenslop hashtag held about 28,000 videos and 379 million views, and US Google searches for the word were rising.

## Tweening vs tweenslop: what is the difference?

Normal **tweening animation** is subtle: a character sways, blinks and shifts its weight, and the in-between frames only make it smooth. **Tweenslop** is the same tool turned all the way up: the whole picture tilts, stretches to twice its height, snaps back and bounces on every kick drum, with color flashes and copies flying in. Nothing is redrawn; it is one picture pushed around as hard as possible.

That contrast became its own tweenslop meme. In "my tweening style vs tweenslop" posts a creator shows a gentle animation first, then the same picture going wild when the funk drops. People act it out in real life too, with a pop tube, a fidget ball, their dog or themselves on a chair.
